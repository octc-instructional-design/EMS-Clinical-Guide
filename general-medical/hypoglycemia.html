Hypoglycemia

Explore the assessment priorities, patient safety considerations, and clinical reasoning questions in the NASEMSO model guideline.

Prototype · Instructor review required

This sample shows how a lengthy clinical guideline could become a more navigable learning experience. It is an educational interpretation, not a replacement for the original protocol.

Clinical safety: This page is not approved for patient care. Students must consult the complete guideline, current local protocols, medical direction, and their scope of practice for treatment decisions, medication routes, and dosing.

1. Recognize the presentation

The NASEMSO hypoglycemia guideline extends beyond patients who already have a low glucose reading. It also identifies presentations that warrant considering a glucose-related problem, including altered consciousness, seizure, stroke-like symptoms, suspected intoxication, and a history of diabetes with other symptoms.

Why it matters: A patient’s initial appearance does not necessarily identify the cause. The guideline prompts clinicians to consider blood glucose as part of a broader assessment rather than relying on one symptom.

Terms to know

  • Altered mental status: A change from the patient’s usual level of awareness or behavior.
  • Focal neurologic deficit: A change affecting a specific neurologic function, such as strength or sensation in one area.
  • Diaphoresis: Sweating; listed among findings that may accompany hypoglycemia.

2. Focus the assessment

The source guideline directs clinicians to check blood glucose and assess mental status and neurologic findings. It also calls attention to an insulin pump, vital-sign abnormalities, dehydration indicators, and evidence of seizure.

Ask yourself

  1. What findings make glucose assessment relevant?
  2. What additional observations help you understand the patient’s condition?
  3. Which findings suggest another guideline may also apply?

Source connection: The hypoglycemia guideline cross-references the Altered Mental Status and Suspected Stroke/Transient Ischemic Attack guidelines. Students should read those linked topics when relevant.

3. Understand the decision points

The original guideline differentiates treatment considerations according to consciousness and ability to protect the airway. It also addresses reassessment, insulin-pump management, continued symptoms, transport, and strict conditions for considering release without transport.

Important distinction: A patient who improves after an intervention is not automatically eligible for release. The source specifies multiple criteria, and it separately directs transport after a hypoglycemia-associated seizure.

Why does reassessment matter?

The guideline calls for reassessment of vital signs and mental status after treatment. It also discusses repeat glucose measurement in specific circumstances and evaluation for other causes when mental status does not normalize.

Why does medication history matter?

The source highlights the risk of recurrent hypoglycemia associated with certain oral diabetes medications, including sulfonylureas. Medication history can therefore influence disposition considerations.

Where are the treatment doses?

Medication doses, concentrations, routes, pediatric distinctions, and insulin-pump instructions remain in the original NASEMSO guideline (printed pages 84–88). They are intentionally not reproduced in this educational prototype; review them against current local protocols with the instructor.

4. Practice: What would you notice?

Patient scenario

You respond to an adult who seems confused and sweaty. A family member says the patient has diabetes and has eaten very little today. You notice an insulin pump.

Before choosing an intervention, consider:

  1. Which details fit the NASEMSO guideline’s inclusion criteria?
  2. What assessments would help clarify the situation?
  3. What would you need to establish about mental status and airway protection?
  4. What information would you want before discussing disposition?
Instructor discussion prompts

Ask learners to locate the relevant assessment, treatment, reassessment, and disposition passages in the source. Have them explain how each finding changes the information needed for a decision. Validate all treatment choices against the applicable local protocol.

5. Check the source

Primary source: National Association of State EMS Officials (NASEMSO), National Model EMS Clinical Guidelines, Version 3.0 (March 2022), “Hypoglycemia,” printed pages 84–88.
